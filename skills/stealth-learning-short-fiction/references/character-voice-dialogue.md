# Character Voice & Dialogue System

This reference is mandatory for any story containing dialogue.

The goal is not merely "natural Japanese." Dialogue must sound like **this specific character speaking to this specific person in this specific moment**.

A line can be grammatically natural and still be wrong for the character. Treat that as a failure.

## Core rule

> Character voice is relational, not just personal.

The same person does not speak the same way to a close friend, a crush, a teacher, a parent, a stranger, a junior, or someone they are angry with.

Before drafting dialogue, create a compact internal voice card for every speaking character.

## 1. Mandatory voice card

Record internally:

- age / school year or life stage
- gender or gender presentation **only when it actually matters to the character's established voice; never use gender stereotypes as a shortcut**
- region / dialect / home language, if established
- relationship to the current listener
- social distance and status difference
- personality in one concrete phrase
- usual energy level
- talkativeness: terse / average / verbose
- directness: blunt / indirect / careful
- teasing level
- politeness level
- first-person pronoun, if needed
- usual second-person address or name use, if needed
- sentence-ending tendencies
- fillers / hesitation habits
- what this character would almost never say
- how speech changes under embarrassment, anger, fear, excitement, or attraction

Do not show this card to the reader unless requested.

## 2. Relationship register overrides generic age register

Do not write "high-school-boy speech" as a single style.

A high school boy talking to his closest male friend may sound completely different from the same boy talking to:

- a girl he likes
- a teacher
- his mother
- a club captain
- a stranger

Likewise, two male high-school friends do not automatically use rough language. Their established personalities and relationship decide the register.

However, avoid polished neutral lines that sound like an adult scriptwriter speaking through a teenager unless the character is actually that composed.

Example of a suspiciously generic line:

> 「どうしたの、そんな顔して」

This **can** be natural for a gentle or soft-spoken character. It is not inherently wrong.

But for an ordinary close male friend in a casual scene, alternatives might naturally be more like:

> 「何その顔」
>
> 「お前どうしたん」
>
> 「なんかあった？」
>
> 「顔死んでるけど」

These are examples, not templates. Choose based on the actual character.

The lesson is: **do not default to neutral, complete, well-shaped Japanese if the relationship would normally produce rougher, shorter, more elliptical speech.**

## 3. Build voice from contrast, not catchphrases

A character does not become distinct because they repeat one ending or verbal tic.

Differentiate voices through a combination of:

- what they notice
- how quickly they answer
- whether they ask or assert
- how much they explain
- whether they tease directly or indirectly
- whether they name feelings
- whether they avoid difficult topics
- word choice
- sentence length
- omission
- silence

Avoid gimmicks such as making one character say the same filler, dialect particle, or catchphrase in every line.

## 4. Dialogue is allowed to be incomplete

Natural conversation is often less complete than written prose.

Characters may:

- omit subjects and objects
- stop halfway
- answer only the part they care about
- ignore a question
- repeat one word
- respond with an action
- change topic to avoid embarrassment

Do not polish every line into a complete sentence.

Bad default:

> 「どうしたの？　何か心配なことがあるように見えるよ」

Potentially more natural depending on voice and relationship:

> 「何。なんかあった？」

or simply:

> 「……お前、今日変じゃね？」

Again, these are not universal replacements.

## 5. Character knowledge limits dialogue

A character can only say what they plausibly know, infer, or misunderstand.

Do not let a friend conveniently name the protagonist's hidden emotion just so the reader understands it.

Prefer:

- observation
- wrong guess
- partial guess
- teasing guess
- silence

over perfect psychological diagnosis.

## 6. Emotion changes register

A character's voice card has a baseline, but emotion modifies it.

### Embarrassed

Possible effects:

- shorter answers
- denial
- topic shift
- unnecessary bluntness
- looking away / action beat instead of speech

### Angry

Possible effects:

- fewer softeners
- shorter clauses
- names replaced by pronouns or omitted
- repeated words

### Nervous

Possible effects:

- over-explaining
- false casualness
- filler
- answering too quickly

### Attracted / romantic tension

Possible effects:

- ordinary sentence becomes difficult to say
- teasing substitutes for directness
- small delay before response
- person notices irrelevant detail

Do not mechanically add stammering or blushing.

## 7. Age realism without parody

Teenagers are not a bag of slang.

Avoid forcing trend words, internet slang, or exaggerated roughness merely to signal youth.

Use contemporary casual Japanese, but let personality and relationship do most of the work.

When uncertain, choose ordinary spoken Japanese over invented youth slang.

## 8. Gender is context, not a dialogue generator

Never generate dialogue from simplistic rules such as:

- boys are rough
- girls are soft
- male friends always say お前
- female friends always use 〜だよね

Gender can interact with identity, social expectations, and character design, but it must not replace actual characterization.

If the character profile does not establish a gendered speech pattern, keep the dialogue natural and individual rather than stereotyped.

## 9. Dialect only when grounded

Do not add Kansai-ben, Hakata-ben, Kitakyushu dialect, or any regional speech merely to make dialogue feel lively.

Use dialect only when the character's region/background is established or the user requests it.

When dialect is used, keep it internally consistent and avoid caricature.

## 10. Relationship-specific address

Track how characters address each other.

Examples:

- surname + さん
- surname only
- first name
- nickname
- お前
- あんた
- 先輩
- 先生
- no address at all

A sudden change in address can be meaningful, so do not change it accidentally.

In romance, a change from surname to first name may carry more weight than an explicit confession. Preserve that possibility.

## 11. The substitution test

After drafting a scene, temporarily swap the speaker labels on several lines.

Ask:

> Could Character A's line be given to Character B with almost no change?

If yes for most dialogue, the voices are too generic.

Revise until at least the important lines depend on who is speaking.

## 12. The listener test

Take one character's line and imagine them saying it to a different listener.

If it remains unchanged despite a large relationship difference, check whether the register is too generic.

Example: a boy may say to his best friend:

> 「いや、それは無理やろ」

but to a teacher:

> 「いや、それはちょっと……」

Exact wording depends on character and dialect. The point is relational modulation.

## 13. The aloud test

Read every dialogue exchange mentally out loud at conversational speed.

Fail and rewrite if:

- a line is too well-structured for the emotion
- a teenager sounds like an explanatory narrator
- both characters use the same rhythm
- a punchline feels authored rather than spontaneous
- an exchange contains no omissions or interpersonal texture where casual speech would normally have them
- a line feels natural on paper but unlikely to leave a person's mouth

## 14. No exposition disguised as dialogue

Automatic warning signs:

- 「つまり〜ということだね」 used to explain the plot
- characters restating facts they both know
- one character asking a question solely so another can explain background
- a friend perfectly summarizing the protagonist's feelings
- every target sentence being introduced by dialogue

Move necessary information into action, implication, brief narration, or cut it.

## 15. English target sentences do not define character voice

Exact English target sentences are learning objects. Do not bend a character's Japanese speech around them.

Unless the story naturally supports English use, keep the Japanese dialogue native and insert the English target separately at the matching story beat with its Japanese translation.

This prevents a character from sounding unnatural merely because the target sentence exists.

## 16. Character Voice QA checklist

For each speaking character, verify:

- [ ] voice card exists internally
- [ ] relationship to each listener is reflected in register
- [ ] address terms are consistent
- [ ] dialogue fits age and status
- [ ] personality is visible without catchphrase gimmicks
- [ ] emotion modifies baseline voice plausibly
- [ ] no unexplained dialect or slang
- [ ] no gender stereotype is doing the characterization
- [ ] important lines fail the substitution test in a good way
- [ ] dialogue passes the aloud test
- [ ] no exposition is disguised as casual speech

If any important dialogue fails, revise before delivery.
