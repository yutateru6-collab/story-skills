# Short-Story Craft for Stealth Learning

Use this reference to keep stories compact, readable, and entertaining even when they contain exact target sentences.

## 1. Start with a live situation

Open on something already happening.

Good openings usually contain at least one of these:

- a problem already in motion
- an odd detail
- a line of dialogue that creates a question
- a small social risk
- a contradiction
- a promise of emotion
- a striking image

Avoid beginning with several paragraphs of background, world history, grammar explanation, or character biography.

Examples of useful opening energies:

- "退部届を出した日のシュートだけ、なぜか入った。"
- "午前二時十三分。死んだはずの姉から通知が来た。"
- "『好きな人できた』と彼女が言った三秒後、僕のスマホが震えた。"

These are craft examples only. Do not reuse them mechanically.

## 2. One story, one central movement

A short story does not need a large plot. It needs a meaningful change.

Useful changes include:

- misunderstanding -> understanding
- hesitation -> decision
- defeat -> renewed effort
- confidence -> doubt
- fear -> worse fear
- distance -> small intimacy
- certainty -> revelation
- resentment -> recognition
- routine -> disruption

Keep the event small enough to complete within the requested length.

For a larger selected target set, extend the same central movement across a few causally linked scenes instead of restarting with a new protagonist or unrelated incident.

## 3. Make the protagonist want something immediately

The protagonist should usually want something concrete in the scene:

- leave before being noticed
- win one point
- delete a message
- hide a mistake
- return an umbrella
- prove a rumor wrong
- survive until morning
- avoid answering a question

Abstract desires such as "be happy" are weak unless converted into a scene-level action.

## 4. Use one pressure source

For short work, one strong pressure is usually better than several weak ones.

Possible pressure sources:

- time limit
- rival
- misunderstanding
- secret
- embarrassment
- physical obstacle
- social expectation
- fear of rejection
- strange phenomenon
- missing information

Do not add a villain, mystery, romance triangle, family secret, and supernatural rule all at once unless the user explicitly asks for a more complex story.

## 5. Compress exposition

Prefer one revealing detail over a paragraph of explanation.

Instead of:

> 彼は三年間ずっとレギュラーになれず、毎朝練習していて、最近は辞めようと思っていた。

Consider staging evidence:

> ロッカーの奥に、三日前に書いた退部届がまだ入っていた。シューズのつま先だけは、今日も泥だらけだった。

Let objects, habits, reactions, and dialogue carry history.

## 6. Dialogue must have friction or character

Avoid dialogue whose only purpose is to explain information the characters already know.

Weak:

> 「君は五年間テニスをしているね」
> 「そうだね。現在完了を使うね」

Better dialogue contains desire, avoidance, teasing, tension, affection, rivalry, fear, or subtext.

Characters should not become teachers unless the story genuinely requires a teacher.

## 7. Use target sentences at emotional or semantic peaks

The exact English sentence is most memorable when it appears near:

- a reveal
- a choice
- a mistake
- a victory or loss
- a confession
- a clue
- a frightening realization
- an important image

Do not place all English at the start or end as a list.

## 8. Vary rhythm

For tension or action:

- shorter sentences
- shorter paragraphs
- concrete verbs
- fewer explanations

For emotion or atmosphere:

- slightly longer sentences
- sensory detail
- pauses
- image-based reflection

Do not write every paragraph at the same length.

## 9. Give the ending a job

A short ending should usually do one of these:

- reframe the opening
- resolve the immediate want
- reveal a hidden fact
- create a small emotional turn
- land a joke
- leave a chilling implication
- show a decision
- echo an earlier image with changed meaning

Avoid ending with a generic moral such as "そして彼は大切なことを学んだ。"

## 10. Protect simplicity without dropping required targets

When the story begins to feel crowded, simplify in this order:

1. extra worldbuilding
2. secondary characters
3. backstory explanation
4. additional twists
5. secondary locations
6. redundant transitions or duplicated emotional explanation

Do **not** remove a source-grounded target from the user's selected range merely to make the story shorter.

If required targets still feel crowded after simplification:

- increase story length within the variable-length guidance
- merge semantically compatible targets into the same incident
- redesign scene order
- use a small number of connected scene breaks

Do not automatically move required targets into another story unless the user asks for multiple stories.

## 11. Five-beat compact structure

Default:

### Hook
Create a question or emotional pull within 1–3 sentences.

### Situation
Clarify what the protagonist wants right now.

### Pressure
Complicate that want.

### Turn
Reveal, decide, fail, succeed, scare, connect, misunderstand, or reverse.

### Aftertaste
End with the genre's desired emotional residue.

The beats may be extremely short. They are functions, not required headings. `Five-beat` refers to story structure, not target-sentence count.

## 12. Final craft check

Before delivery, ask internally:

- Can the story be summarized as one interesting incident?
- Is the first paragraph stronger than background exposition?
- Does every major paragraph either move the incident or deepen emotion/atmosphere?
- Does something change by the end?
- Does emotional investment accumulate instead of reset?
- Are all targets in the selected range naturally integrated?
- Is the last line specific rather than generic?
- Would a student plausibly finish this even if there were no test tomorrow?
