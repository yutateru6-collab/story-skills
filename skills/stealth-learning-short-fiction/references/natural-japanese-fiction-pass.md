# Natural Japanese Fiction Pass

This pass exists because grammatically correct Japanese can still feel artificial, translated, over-written, or "AI-novel-like." Apply it to every Japanese story before delivery.

It complements `japanese-prose-style.md`. The general language principles are adapted from the owner's `natural-japanese` skill, especially its guidance on translationese, Japanese word order, readability, sentence-ending rhythm, and anti-AI revision. Fiction needs additional controls for dialogue, inner monologue, comic timing, and emotional restraint.

## Goal

The finished Japanese should feel as though a native Japanese writer wrote this particular scene for this particular character.

Do not aim for "light-novel flavor" as a collection of surface tricks. Aim for believable Japanese first. Genre flavor comes after that.

## 1. Native-speech test comes before cleverness

For every line of dialogue and first-person inner monologue, ask:

> Would a Japanese person of this age, relationship, mood, and setting plausibly say or think this exact line?

If the answer is "maybe, because it sounds witty," rewrite it more plainly.

Prefer ordinary words used precisely over conspicuous phrases written to sound literary, funny, youthful, or dramatic.

Do not force every exchange to be sharp banter. Real conversations contain:

- short answers
- unfinished thoughts
- mild repetition
- silence
- topic shifts
- ordinary reactions
- action instead of verbal response

Use these selectively, not mechanically.

## 2. Ban pseudo-light-novel choppiness

Short sentences are useful for impact. A chain of sentence fragments is not automatically natural.

Avoid rhythms such as:

> パリ。フランス。遠い。なんかおしゃれ。

unless the character voice and moment genuinely justify the fragmentation.

Warning pattern:

- noun fragment
- noun fragment
- one-clause reaction
- another fragment
- punchline

repeated several times in the same paragraph.

Default to normal Japanese connective flow when thoughts belong together.

Before:

> パリ。フランス。遠い。なんかおしゃれ。簡単には会えない場所だ。

Better:

> パリに行くらしい。たった数日の旅行だと分かっているのに、妙に遠くへ行ってしまう気がした。

The better version is not mandatory wording. The rule is to connect thought where Japanese normally connects thought.

## 3. Do not manufacture a punchline every paragraph

AI fiction often tries to end each paragraph with a witty metaphor, self-deprecating joke, or quotable line.

Do not add lines such as:

- 「今すぐ○○になりたい」
- 「完全に俺の負けだった」
- 「心臓が仕事を放棄した」
- 「その一言で世界が止まった」

unless the character voice has earned that expression and it fits the scene.

One memorable line is stronger than five lines competing to be memorable.

When revising, delete at least one "clever" line if removing it makes the scene feel more human.

## 4. Avoid explanatory inner monologue

Do not make the viewpoint character explain to the reader what the scene has already shown.

Bad pattern:

1. event happens
2. character reacts
3. inner monologue restates the event
4. inner monologue explains why the reaction is reasonable
5. another line summarizes the emotion

Stop after the strongest one or two layers.

Readers do not need every implication interpreted for them.

## 5. Japanese word order, omission, and subject handling

Japanese normally omits information that is obvious from context.

Avoid unnecessary repetition of:

- 私は / 僕は / 俺は
- 彼は / 彼女は
- names in consecutive sentences
- explicit objects already understood

Keep subject and predicate relationships clear, but do not preserve English-style explicit subjects merely for completeness.

Prefer Japanese information order over English syntax. If a sentence feels like an English sentence translated clause-by-clause, rebuild it from the Japanese situation rather than patching words.

Typical translationese to avoid unless context requires it:

- heavy use of 無生物主語 + 他動詞
- nested noun modification
- 「それは〜だ。なぜなら〜」 when ordinary Japanese would join the reason directly
- 「〜することができる」 where a natural potential form works
- 「〜することによって」 where 「〜すると／〜して」 works
- abstract `have`-like expressions such as 「意味を持つ」 when 「意味がある」 is more natural
- double em-dash insertions copied from English prose

## 6. Sentence endings must vary naturally, not cosmetically

Do not mechanically alternate endings only to avoid repetition.

Check clusters of three or more nearby sentences ending in the same pattern, especially:

- 〜だった。
- 〜した。
- 〜いる。
- 〜と思った。
- 〜気がした。

Fix the underlying syntax or combine sentences when needed.

Natural variation can come from:

- dialogue
- noun-ending fragments used sparingly
- subordinate clauses
- action beats
- sentence merging
- omission

Do not replace one repetitive ending with an equally artificial sequence of stylistic tricks.

## 7. Punctuation follows syntax, not performance

Japanese commas mark structural boundaries. Do not scatter commas simply to create dramatic pauses.

Use ellipses and dashes sparingly.

Avoid overusing:

- 「……」 in every exchange
- 「――」 for ordinary interruption or emphasis
- repeated exclamation/question marks
- one-line paragraphs solely to make ordinary prose look dramatic

A quiet scene should be allowed to read quietly.

## 8. Dialogue must fit relationship distance

Before finalizing dialogue, identify internally:

- age
- relative status
- closeness
- whether they normally joke
- whether they are embarrassed, angry, tired, formal, or guarded

Then check pronouns, sentence endings, honorifics, teasing intensity, and directness.

Do not make every teenage character speak in the same neutral anime voice.

Do not make a shy character suddenly deliver a perfectly shaped confession simply because the scene needs closure.

Do not make friends explain shared history to each other for reader convenience.

## 9. Romance requires subtext, not constant self-commentary

For romantic comedy and romance:

Prefer:

- timing
- hesitation
- a changed distance
- an ordinary object becoming meaningful
- noticing something the character usually ignores
- teasing whose meaning is slightly ambiguous
- a small action that can be read two ways

Avoid repeatedly stating:

- 「かわいいと思った」
- 「ドキドキした」
- 「顔が熱くなった」
- 「好きなのかもしれない」
- 「これは恋だ」

Direct emotion is allowed, but do not narrate every romantic signal.

Rom-com dialogue should not become a sequence of prepared punchlines. Let one person miss, misunderstand, hesitate, or answer plainly.

## 10. Horror requires restraint

For horror, do not explain the scary implication after the reader can already see it.

Prefer one concrete wrong detail over several adjectives about fear.

Do not end with a paragraph explaining why the event was terrifying.

## 11. Sports fiction requires physical truth

For sports stories, use concrete action and pressure rather than generic motivational phrases.

Avoid:

- 「努力は裏切らない」 unless a character would actually say it
- abstract declarations about dreams without scene evidence
- sudden skill improvement solely for payoff

Use breath, timing, missed movement, score, distance, repetition, fatigue, equipment, or body sensation where appropriate.

## 12. English target blocks must not distort Japanese scene logic

The story is Japanese. Do not invent an implausible reason for Japanese characters to suddenly write or speak an exact English target sentence.

Default handling when no natural English-speaking context exists:

1. tell the scene naturally in Japanese
2. insert the target English as a clearly separated learning block at the semantically matching moment
3. place its Japanese translation directly below
4. continue the Japanese story

The target block does not need to be diegetic dialogue, a chat message, or something the character literally said.

Use diegetic English only when it is naturally justified by the story: English class, international setting, foreign character, announcement, article, signage, actual message, etc.

Never damage scene plausibility merely to "explain why English appears."

## 13. Translation under the target should be Japanese, not translationese

When the supplied Japanese translation must be reproduced exactly for test memorization, preserve it.

When natural translation is permitted, prefer ordinary Japanese that preserves the target meaning.

Do not make the surrounding prose mimic the syntax of the English sentence.

The translation is a learning aid, not a model for the narrative voice.

## 14. Read-aloud pass

Before delivery, read the Japanese mentally as continuous speech.

Mark any place where:

- the mouth trips over the word order
- two adjacent sentences repeat the same cadence
- dialogue sounds written rather than spoken
- a joke feels inserted by the author rather than arising from the character
- a sentence feels too complete for an emotional moment
- the narrator explains what the reader already knows

Rewrite those spots.

Do not judge sentence by sentence only. Naturalness is often a paragraph-level rhythm issue.

## 15. Remove-AI pass

After the read-aloud pass, scan specifically for these AI-fiction tendencies:

- too many short dramatic paragraphs
- too many rhetorical questions in inner monologue
- self-answering questions
- perfectly symmetrical exchanges
- convenient witty retorts
- generic metaphor about heart/world/time
- repeated 「なぜか」「妙に」「不思議と」「気がした」
- repeated 「その瞬間」「その一言で」
- moral or emotional summary after the actual ending has already landed
- a final "quotable" line that explains the theme

Remove or simplify them unless the story specifically earns them.

## 16. Native rewrite rule

When a passage feels unnatural, do not preserve its sentence structure and replace synonyms.

Reconstruct from the situation:

- What just happened?
- What would this character notice?
- What would they actually say or leave unsaid?
- What information does the reader already have?

Then write the Japanese again from zero.

This is especially important for passages influenced by English target sentences.

## Final pass/fail questions

A story fails the natural-Japanese pass if any of these are true:

- several lines sound like translated English syntax
- teenage dialogue sounds like a script explaining the plot
- the narrator repeatedly performs cleverness
- sentence fragments are used as a default rhythm
- emotional meaning is explained after it is already clear
- characters speak English only because a target sentence must appear
- multiple characters share the same voice
- the final paragraph states the story's emotional lesson

When in doubt, choose the quieter and more ordinary Japanese phrasing.
